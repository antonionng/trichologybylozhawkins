# Lesson 1: The scalp conditions you'll see most often

**Lesson slug:** scalp-conditions  
**videoUrl:** courses/salon-trichology-essentials/lessons/scalp-conditions/vo.mp4  
**Word count estimate:** ~720 words (~4.5-5.5 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Stylists observe and describe; they do not diagnose medical disease.
- Same symptom can have different causes; when in doubt, refer.
- Product choice supports comfort and management; it is not a medical treatment.

**Script body:**

You are in the chair. The cape is on, the client is settling, and before you reach for the shampoo or the scissors, you have a quiet window to look. That look is the start of good trichology in the salon. Not diagnosis. Observation.

In salon practice, a handful of scalp pictures come up again and again. Learning to recognise them, and to talk about them calmly, is what this lesson is for. You will leave with a mental checklist you can run in under a minute, without frightening anyone and without overclaiming.

First, the sensitive or inflamed scalp. The client may say it feels tight, hot, itchy, or sore. You might see redness, flaking that sits on the scalp rather than loose dandruff-style flakes, or an area that looks shiny and irritated where products or heat have landed heavily. Your job is not to name a medical condition. Your job is to notice, to soften your language, and to avoid anything that could make the discomfort worse while you work. Lukewarm water, fewer layered actives, and a lighter massage pressure are simple adjustments. If the skin looks broken, weeping, or rapidly spreading, that is a stop-and-refer moment, not a product experiment.

Second, the flaky scalp. Flakes can be dry and powdery, or oily and stuck in clumps. Clients often call everything "dandruff", but what you see and feel matters more than the label. Dry, sparse flakes with a tight feel suggest one approach. Greasy, yellow-tinged scale with an oily mid-lengths picture suggests another. You can describe what you see in plain English: "I'm noticing some build-up at the scalp and a bit of oil through the mid-lengths." That kind of language builds trust without pretending you have made a medical diagnosis. If flake comes with thick plaques, bleeding, or pain, step back from retail stacking and refer.

Third, oiliness and product build-up. Not every shiny scalp is a disease. Frequent silicone-heavy styling, infrequent clarifying, or a very fine hair type can leave the scalp looking coated. The client may complain that styles fall flat or that the scalp feels itchy even when it does not look inflamed. Clarify gently when appropriate, and recommend a routine they can keep. Do not promise to "rebalance hormones" or "detox the scalp". Keep it practical: cleanse quality, frequency that fits their life, and lighter products at the root.

Fourth, traction clues. Look along the hairline, the crown parting, and areas where ponytails, braids, weaves, or tight updos sit day after day. Thinning at the temples, broken short hairs in a band, or tenderness when you touch the scalp are soft signals. You can say, "I'm seeing some stress along this hairline. Looser tension and rotating styles would be kinder while we keep an eye on it." If there is clear baldness in a patterned pull zone and the client is worried, refer rather than reassure blindly. Traction can sit alongside other thinning pictures, so stay humble about cause.

Fifth, the "itchy but nothing to see" presentation. Sometimes the scalp looks quiet and the client still reports itch, crawl, or burn. Note it. Soften fragrance and actives. Avoid aggressive scrubbing. Check whether colour, bleach, or new styling products arrived just before the symptoms. If symptoms persist beyond a short trial of gentler care, guide them toward a trichologist or GP rather than stacking more retail products.

Through all of this, hold one rule firmly. You are a stylist with trained eyes and a professional voice. You are not diagnosing psoriasis, eczema, infection, or autoimmune disease from the chair. You can say what you observe. You can adjust the service. You can recommend supportive products within your training. And you can refer when the picture is unclear, severe, or outside your scope. Referral is not a lost sale. It is how you keep clients safe and keep your reputation clean.

Practical takeaway for today: before you shampoo, take ten quiet seconds to look at the scalp in sections. Name what you see in plain language. Soften the service if the scalp looks sensitive. And if anything looks broken, spreading, or medically uncertain, pause the experiment and refer.
