# Lesson 1: The scalp conditions you'll see most often

**Word count estimate:** ~720 words (~4.5-5.5 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Stylists observe and describe; they do not diagnose medical disease.
- Same symptom can have different causes; when in doubt, refer.
- Product choice supports comfort and management; it is not a medical treatment.

**Script body:**

You are in the chair. The cape is on, the client is settling, and before you reach for the shampoo or the scissors, you have a quiet window to look. That look is the start of good trichology in the salon. Not diagnosis. Observation.

In salon practice, a handful of scalp pictures come up again and again. Learning to recognise them, and to talk about them calmly, is what this lesson is for. You will leave with a mental checklist you can run in under a minute, without frightening anyone and without overclaiming.

First, the sensitive or inflamed scalp. The client may say it feels tight, hot, itchy, or sore. You might see redness, flaking that sits on the scalp rather than loose dandruff-style flakes, or an area that looks shiny and irritated where products or heat have landed heavily. Your job is not to name a medical condition. Your job is to notice, to soften your language, and to avoid anything that could make the discomfort worse while you work. Lukewarm water, fewer layered actives, and a lighter massage pressure are simple adjustments. If the skin looks broken, weeping, or rapidly spreading, that is a stop-and-refer moment, not a product experiment.

Second, the flaky scalp. Flakes can be dry and powdery, or oily and stuck in clumps. Clients often call everything "dandruff", but what you see and feel matters more than the label. Dry, sparse flakes with a tight feel suggest one approach. Greasy, yellow-tinged scale with an oily mid-lengths picture suggests another. You can describe what you see in plain English: "I'm noticing some build-up at the scalp and a bit of oil through the mid-lengths." That kind of language builds trust without pretending you have made a medical diagnosis. If flake comes with thick plaques, bleeding, or pain, step back from retail stacking and refer.

Third, oiliness and product build-up. Not every shiny scalp is a disease. Frequent silicone-heavy styling, infrequent clarifying, or a very fine hair type can leave the scalp looking coated. The client may complain that styles fall flat or that the scalp feels itchy even when it does not look inflamed. Clarify gently when appropriate, and recommend a routine they can keep. Do not promise to "rebalance hormones" or "detox the scalp". Keep it practical: cleanse quality, frequency that fits their life, and lighter products at the root.

Fourth, traction clues. Look along the hairline, the crown parting, and areas where ponytails, braids, weaves, or tight updos sit day after day. Thinning at the temples, broken short hairs in a band, or tenderness when you touch the scalp are soft signals. You can say, "I'm seeing some stress along this hairline. Looser tension and rotating styles would be kinder while we keep an eye on it." If there is clear baldness in a patterned pull zone and the client is worried, refer rather than reassure blindly. Traction can sit alongside other thinning pictures, so stay humble about cause.

Fifth, the "itchy but nothing to see" presentation. Sometimes the scalp looks quiet and the client still reports itch, crawl, or burn. Note it. Soften fragrance and actives. Avoid aggressive scrubbing. Check whether colour, bleach, or new styling products arrived just before the symptoms. If symptoms persist beyond a short trial of gentler care, guide them toward a trichologist or GP rather than stacking more retail products.

Through all of this, hold one rule firmly. You are a stylist with trained eyes and a professional voice. You are not diagnosing psoriasis, eczema, infection, or autoimmune disease from the chair. You can say what you observe. You can adjust the service. You can recommend supportive products within your training. And you can refer when the picture is unclear, severe, or outside your scope. Referral is not a lost sale. It is how you keep clients safe and keep your reputation clean.

Practical takeaway for today: before you shampoo, take ten quiet seconds to look at the scalp in sections. Name what you see in plain language. Soften the service if the scalp looks sensitive. And if anything looks broken, spreading, or medically uncertain, pause the experiment and refer.

# Lesson 2: When hair loss is more than normal shedding

**Word count estimate:** ~780 words (~5-6 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Shedding, breakage, pattern thinning, and patchy loss are different pictures; do not collapse them into one story.
- Postpartum lag and telogen effluvium are common salon conversations; still refer when sudden, severe, or accompanied by scalp or health red flags.
- Stylists do not diagnose androgenetic alopecia or other medical hair loss conditions; they recognise patterns and refer appropriately.

**Script body:**

Every stylist has heard it. "I'm losing so much hair. Is this normal?" The answer is never a shrug and never a miracle promise. It is a careful look at what kind of loss you are seeing, and a calm conversation about what belongs in the salon and what belongs with a clinician.

Start by separating four pictures: shed, breakage, pattern thinning, and patchy loss. Clients often mix these words. Your clarity helps them feel less alone and helps you choose safer next steps.

Shed is hair coming out from the root. You see bulbs on the ends in the brush or on the cape. The scalp may look generally full, or slightly thinner through density, without sharp bald patches. Everyday shedding happens. A noticeable increase after illness, stress, crash dieting, medication change, or childbirth is something clients often describe weeks to months later. That delayed pattern is why postpartum lag comes up so often. The birth was months ago; the shed is peaking now. In training language you will hear telogen effluvium, or TE, for a diffuse shed picture linked to a trigger. In the chair, prefer observational language. You can acknowledge timing without diagnosing TE yourself. You can also say that sudden, severe, or ongoing shed with other symptoms deserves a professional opinion beyond the salon.

Breakage is different. Short, snapped lengths without a bulb, a rough cuticle feel, and damage concentrated where heat, bleach, or mechanical stress land. Density at the scalp can look fine while the ends look thin and frayed. Treating breakage as "hair loss disease" confuses the client and wastes time. Adjust chemistry, heat, and handling. Recommend repair-minded care. Refer if you also see true root shed or pattern change that does not match the breakage story.

Pattern thinning is the slow, patterned reduction many clients notice at the parting, crown, or temples. In women, a widening centre parting or see-through crown is a common chair complaint. In men, recession and crown thinning are familiar. Androgenetic alopecia, often shortened to AGA, is the clinical name you will hear in training materials. In the salon, prefer observational language: "I'm noticing the parting looks a little wider than last time," or "density through the crown feels finer." Do not tell a client they "have AGA" as a firm diagnosis from the chair. You can discuss supportive styling, gentle colour strategy, and when a trichology or medical review would help. Be honest that salon products support appearance and hair integrity; they are not a cure for patterned loss.

Patchy loss is a red flag picture. Smooth round or irregular bald patches, sudden bare areas, broken hairs in a localised zone, or loss with pain, scale, or pustules. Stop guessing. Soften the service if needed for comfort, document what you see in your notes in plain language, and refer. Patchy loss can have several causes. Sorting them is not a stylist's job.

Also watch for traction clues mixed with thinning. Continuous tight styles can contribute to edge loss that looks patterned but is linked to pull. Loosen, rotate, and refer if the loss is established or the client is distressed.

When should you stop and refer? When shed is sudden and heavy. When patches appear. When the scalp is inflamed, painful, or broken. When the client reports other health changes they are worried about. When you are simply unsure. Referral is not failure. It is professional care.

Practical takeaway: next time a client says they are losing hair, ask yourself which picture you are seeing. Shed, breakage, pattern, or patch. Speak to that picture in plain English. Support what you can in the salon. Refer what you should not manage alone.

# Lesson 3: Starting the conversation

**Word count estimate:** ~650 words (~4-5 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Lead with observation and permission; never alarm or diagnose from the chair.
- Consent and privacy matter; keep conversations proportionate to the service.
- Offer referral as support, not as a scare.

**Script body:**

Trust in the salon starts with how you open the subject. Many clients already feel embarrassed about flake, itch, or thinning. Your tone can lower the temperature or raise it in a single sentence. This lesson is about that first minute, when a small choice of words decides whether the client leans in or shuts down.

Begin with permission and observation. "Would it be alright if I mention something I'm noticing on the scalp?" is softer than "You've got a problem." Once they agree, describe what you see without medical labels you are not qualified to assign. "I'm seeing some redness along the parting," or "There's a bit more shed on the cape than last visit," keeps you in scope and invites their story. If they say no, respect that. You can still adapt the service quietly for comfort.

Then listen. Ask one or two open questions. "How long have you been noticing that?" "Has anything changed recently with health, stress, colour, or styling?" You are gathering context for a safer service and a kinder recommendation, not conducting a medical interview. Keep your face calm. Do not grimace at the scalp. Clients read your expression faster than your script. If they disclose something clearly clinical, thank them for telling you and keep the next step simple: salon adjustments today, and a referral pathway if needed.

Match your language to the picture. For sensitivity: "We'll keep today's service gentle and avoid anything that might sting." For flakes: "We can look at a clearer cleanse and a routine that doesn't sit heavy on the scalp." For shed: "Some increase in shed can follow stress or illness a couple of months later. If it's heavy or patchy, I'd rather you had it checked properly." For pattern thinning: "I'm noticing the parting looks a little wider. We can style and colour in a way that is kinder to the scalp, and I can point you toward a clinical review if you want clearer answers." For traction: "The edges look under strain. Looser styles would be kinder while we monitor this."

Avoid three traps. First, the miracle trap: "This product will grow it all back." Second, the diagnosis trap: naming psoriasis, alopecia areata, or thyroid disease from a glance. Third, the dismissal trap: "It's nothing, don't worry," when the client is clearly worried or the picture looks atypical. Calm honesty sits between those extremes. You can validate feeling without inventing certainty.

Privacy matters. Keep the conversation at the chair proportionate. Do not announce scalp concerns across the salon floor. If a fuller talk is needed, offer a quieter moment or a follow-up message through your salon's usual channels. Notes should stay factual and kind.

Close the opening well. Summarise what you will do today, what they can try at home within reason, and when you would suggest seeing a trichologist or GP. Mentioning education and clinic pathways through The Trichology Academy loop is fine when referral fits. Loz sees clients in Knutsford; the Academy itself is education for professionals. Keep that distinction clear so clients are not confused about what they are booking. A useful closing line is: "I'll look after what we can safely do here, and I'll help you find the right next step if this needs more than salon care."

Practical takeaway: open with permission, describe what you see, ask a short question, then agree the next kind step. That sequence builds trust without overstepping.

# Lesson 4: Recommending products and services with confidence

**Word count estimate:** ~680 words (~4.5-5.5 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Recommendations support comfort, manageability, and appearance; they are not medical treatment or cure claims.
- Match product and service to the observed picture; less is often safer on inflamed scalps.
- Know when to stop selling and start referring.

**Script body:**

Recommendation is where education meets the till, and it is also where trust can be lost. Confident does not mean pushy. Confident means matched to what you saw, honest about limits, and clear about what a product or service can and cannot do. Clients can feel the difference between care and a pitch.

Anchor every recommendation in the chair observation. If the scalp looked sensitive, lead with fragrance-light cleansing, lukewarm water, and fewer stacked actives. If the picture was oily build-up, talk about clarifying cadence and lighter leave-ins at the root. If breakage dominated, prioritise bond-minded or conditioning strategies through the lengths and gentler heat. If pattern thinning was the concern, discuss styling for density appearance, colour placement that does not overexpose the scalp, and realistic timelines. Never sell a bottle as a cure for medical hair loss. Never imply that a salon ritual replaces a clinician when patches, pain, or sudden heavy shed are present.

Use structured language. "Based on what I'm seeing, I'd suggest… The reason is… What it won't do is…" That third line protects you and the client. It also reduces refunds and disappointment. Example: "I'd suggest a gentler cleanse and a lighter root routine. The reason is the scalp looks easily irritated today. What it won't do is treat an underlying medical condition if one is present."

Service recommendations follow the same logic. A soothing scalp service may help comfort on a non-broken, non-infected looking scalp. A clarifying service may help product-coated roots. A restorative lengths service may help breakage. A precision cut and colour strategy may help the appearance of density. None of these replace medical care for patchy loss, painful inflammation, or sudden severe shed. If the scalp is broken, pustular, or the loss is patchy, pause retail energy and refer.

Be careful with growth, regrowth, and "anti-hair-loss" language. Prefer: supports the appearance of density, helps reduce breakage, eases the feel of dryness or itch for some clients, or fits a gentler routine while they seek clinical advice. British clients are often wary of hype. Clinical calm sells better than theatre. If a brand claim feels stronger than your observation, tone it down in your own words.

Bundle only what earns its place. A shampoo plus a length treatment can be coherent. Five products for one mild flake complaint usually is not. Explain order of use in one short sentence. Write it down if that helps them. Check back at the next appointment: what helped, what irritated, what they actually used. That follow-through is part of confidence.

When money and ethics meet, choose the long relationship. If the right answer is "not today, let's get this checked," say that. You can still book their next cut, adjust today's service, and leave the door open. Referral through the Academy education loop and, where appropriate, clinic pathways linked via trichologyacademy.co.uk keeps the professional standard visible. Knutsford clinic care sits with Loz as clinician; your role remains salon observation, language, and support.

Practical takeaway: recommend from observation, state the limit in one plain sentence, and stop selling when referral is the safer next step.

# Lesson 5: Scope of practice for stylists

**Word count estimate:** ~700 words (~4.5-5.5 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Stylists observe, adapt services, educate lightly, and refer; they do not diagnose or treat medical disease.
- Red flags mean stop, document plainly, and refer.
- Referral is part of professionalism, not an admission of failure.

**Script body:**

Scope of practice is the quiet backbone of salon trichology. It tells you what you can do well, and what you must hand on. Clients trust you with their appearance and often with their worries. Honouring that trust means knowing your lane, especially on busy Saturdays when speed tempts shortcuts.

Inside scope for a stylist trained at this Essentials level: careful visual and tactile observation of hair and scalp during normal services; plain-language description of what you see; adjusting water temperature, product choice, tension, and chemical plans for comfort and hair integrity; recognising common pictures such as sensitive scalp, flake and build-up, traction clues, increased shed versus breakage, pattern thinning versus patchy loss; recommending supportive products and services without cure claims; and guiding clients toward appropriate professional help when needed. You can also educate colleagues gently so the whole salon speaks with one careful voice.

Outside scope: diagnosing medical conditions; interpreting blood tests; prescribing or advising prescription medicines; claiming to cure alopecia, psoriasis, infection, or autoimmune disease; continuing invasive or irritating services on a broken or severely inflamed scalp; and dismissing red flags to protect a retail sale. If a client asks, "So what have I got?" a safe reply is, "I can tell you what I'm seeing and how we'll adapt today. Naming a medical condition needs a clinician."

Red flags to stop and refer include patchy bald areas, sudden severe shed, painful or weeping scalp, pustules or signs that could suggest infection, rapidly spreading inflammation, unexplained scarring appearance, and any situation where you feel out of your depth. You do not need certainty about the cause. Uncertainty itself is a reason to refer. When red flags appear mid-service, prioritise comfort, stop aggravating steps, and explain the pause without drama.

How to refer well. Be specific and kind. "I'd like you to have this checked by a trichologist or your GP. I can keep your salon care gentle in the meantime." Offer a clear pathway when you have one. Through The Trichology Academy loop, education sits at trichologyacademy.co.uk, and clinic care with Loz is available in Knutsford for clients who need that step. Keep the distinction tidy: Academy trains professionals; clinic assesses clients. You are not abandoning the client. You are widening the team around them.

Document briefly in your client notes using observation words, not diagnostic labels you cannot stand behind. Note what you adapted in the service and what advice you gave about referral. That protects continuity if a colleague sees them next, and it shows you took the concern seriously.

Emotionally, some stylists fear referral will lose the client. In practice, clear boundaries often deepen trust. Clients remember who took them seriously. They also remember who overpromised. Your professional identity after this course is not "junior doctor with scissors." It is a stylist who can see more, say less that is harmful, and act sooner when the chair is not enough. That is enough to change the standard of care on a salon floor full of rushed conversations.

Practical takeaway: memorise your in-scope list and your red-flag list. When a case sits on the line, choose referral and gentle salon support over guesswork.
