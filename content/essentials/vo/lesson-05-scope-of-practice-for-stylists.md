# Lesson 5: Scope of practice for stylists

**Lesson slug:** scope-of-practice-for-stylists  
**videoUrl:** courses/salon-trichology-essentials/lessons/scope-of-practice-for-stylists/vo.mp4  
**Word count estimate:** ~700 words (~4.5-5.5 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Stylists observe, adapt services, educate lightly, and refer; they do not diagnose or treat medical disease.
- Red flags mean stop, document plainly, and refer.
- Referral is part of professionalism, not an admission of failure.

**Script body:**

Scope of practice is the quiet backbone of salon trichology. It tells you what you can do well, and what you must hand on. Clients trust you with their appearance and often with their worries. Honouring that trust means knowing your lane, especially on busy Saturdays when speed tempts shortcuts.

Inside scope for a stylist trained at this Essentials level: careful visual and tactile observation of hair and scalp during normal services; plain-language description of what you see; adjusting water temperature, product choice, tension, and chemical plans for comfort and hair integrity; recognising common pictures such as sensitive scalp, flake and build-up, traction clues, increased shed versus breakage, pattern thinning versus patchy loss; recommending supportive products and services without cure claims; and guiding clients toward appropriate professional help when needed. You can also educate colleagues gently so the whole salon speaks with one careful voice.

Outside scope: diagnosing medical conditions; interpreting blood tests; prescribing or advising prescription medicines; claiming to cure alopecia, psoriasis, infection, or autoimmune disease; continuing invasive or irritating services on a broken or severely inflamed scalp; and dismissing red flags to protect a retail sale. If a client asks, "So what have I got?" a safe reply is, "I can tell you what I'm seeing and how we'll adapt today. Naming a medical condition needs a clinician."

Red flags to stop and refer include patchy bald areas, sudden severe shed, painful or weeping scalp, pustules or signs that could suggest infection, rapidly spreading inflammation, unexplained scarring appearance, and any situation where you feel out of your depth. You do not need certainty about the cause. Uncertainty itself is a reason to refer. When red flags appear mid-service, prioritise comfort, stop aggravating steps, and explain the pause without drama.

How to refer well. Be specific and kind. "I'd like you to have this checked by a trichologist or your GP. I can keep your salon care gentle in the meantime." Offer a clear pathway when you have one. Through The Trichology Academy loop, education sits at trichologyacademy.co.uk, and clinic care with Loz is available in Knutsford for clients who need that step. Keep the distinction tidy: Academy trains professionals; clinic assesses clients. You are not abandoning the client. You are widening the team around them.

Document briefly in your client notes using observation words, not diagnostic labels you cannot stand behind. Note what you adapted in the service and what advice you gave about referral. That protects continuity if a colleague sees them next, and it shows you took the concern seriously.

Emotionally, some stylists fear referral will lose the client. In practice, clear boundaries often deepen trust. Clients remember who took them seriously. They also remember who overpromised. Your professional identity after this course is not "junior doctor with scissors." It is a stylist who can see more, say less that is harmful, and act sooner when the chair is not enough. That is enough to change the standard of care on a salon floor full of rushed conversations.

Practical takeaway: memorise your in-scope list and your red-flag list. When a case sits on the line, choose referral and gentle salon support over guesswork.
