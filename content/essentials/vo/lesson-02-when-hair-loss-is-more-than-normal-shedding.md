# Lesson 2: When hair loss is more than normal shedding

**Lesson slug:** when-hair-loss-is-more-than-normal-shedding  
**videoUrl:** courses/salon-trichology-essentials/lessons/when-hair-loss-is-more-than-normal-shedding/vo.mp4  
**Word count estimate:** ~780 words (~5-6 min spoken)

**Must-say caveats:**
- Shedding, breakage, pattern thinning, and patchy loss are different pictures; do not collapse them into one story.
- Postpartum lag and telogen effluvium are common salon conversations; still refer when sudden, severe, or accompanied by scalp or health red flags.
- Stylists do not diagnose androgenetic alopecia or other medical hair loss conditions; they recognise patterns and refer appropriately.

**Script body:**

Every stylist has heard it. "I'm losing so much hair. Is this normal?" The answer is never a shrug and never a miracle promise. It is a careful look at what kind of loss you are seeing, and a calm conversation about what belongs in the salon and what belongs with a clinician.

Start by separating four pictures: shed, breakage, pattern thinning, and patchy loss. Clients often mix these words. Your clarity helps them feel less alone and helps you choose safer next steps.

Shed is hair coming out from the root. You see bulbs on the ends in the brush or on the cape. The scalp may look generally full, or slightly thinner through density, without sharp bald patches. Everyday shedding happens. A noticeable increase after illness, stress, crash dieting, medication change, or childbirth is something clients often describe weeks to months later. That delayed pattern is why postpartum lag comes up so often. The birth was months ago; the shed is peaking now. In training language you will hear telogen effluvium, or TE, for a diffuse shed picture linked to a trigger. In the chair, prefer observational language. You can acknowledge timing without diagnosing TE yourself. You can also say that sudden, severe, or ongoing shed with other symptoms deserves a professional opinion beyond the salon.

Breakage is different. Short, snapped lengths without a bulb, a rough cuticle feel, and damage concentrated where heat, bleach, or mechanical stress land. Density at the scalp can look fine while the ends look thin and frayed. Treating breakage as "hair loss disease" confuses the client and wastes time. Adjust chemistry, heat, and handling. Recommend repair-minded care. Refer if you also see true root shed or pattern change that does not match the breakage story.

Pattern thinning is the slow, patterned reduction many clients notice at the parting, crown, or temples. In women, a widening centre parting or see-through crown is a common chair complaint. In men, recession and crown thinning are familiar. Androgenetic alopecia, often shortened to AGA, is the clinical name you will hear in training materials. In the salon, prefer observational language: "I'm noticing the parting looks a little wider than last time," or "density through the crown feels finer." Do not tell a client they "have AGA" as a firm diagnosis from the chair. You can discuss supportive styling, gentle colour strategy, and when a trichology or medical review would help. Be honest that salon products support appearance and hair integrity; they are not a cure for patterned loss.

Patchy loss is a red flag picture. Smooth round or irregular bald patches, sudden bare areas, broken hairs in a localised zone, or loss with pain, scale, or pustules. Stop guessing. Soften the service if needed for comfort, document what you see in your notes in plain language, and refer. Patchy loss can have several causes. Sorting them is not a stylist's job.

Also watch for traction clues mixed with thinning. Continuous tight styles can contribute to edge loss that looks patterned but is linked to pull. Loosen, rotate, and refer if the loss is established or the client is distressed.

When should you stop and refer? When shed is sudden and heavy. When patches appear. When the scalp is inflamed, painful, or broken. When the client reports other health changes they are worried about. When you are simply unsure. Referral is not failure. It is professional care.

Practical takeaway: next time a client says they are losing hair, ask yourself which picture you are seeing. Shed, breakage, pattern, or patch. Speak to that picture in plain English. Support what you can in the salon. Refer what you should not manage alone.
